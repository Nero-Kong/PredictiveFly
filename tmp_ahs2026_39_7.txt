1 Introduction
Virtual reality (VR) provides new opportunities for extending hu-
man capabilities beyond physical limitations, enabling experiences
that are unattainable in the physical world. Among these, free three-
dimensional (3D) flight stands out as a notable form of human
augmentation. Flying allows users to move fluidly through space,
observe environments from novel vantage points, and traverse large
or multi-layered structures with ease[24, 30]. Such capabilities are
valuable across domains including spatial exploration, scientific vi-
sualization, environmental education, and creative design. In these
scenarios, users must often navigate while continuously inspecting
their surroundings, making it essential to support movement and
free visual exploration simultaneously.
Most existing VR systems implement 3D movement and flight
using abstract input mappings such as joysticks, buttons, or thumb-
sticks. Although easy to implement and broadly compatible, these
mappings lack body-related motion cues and do not align with
users’ natural sensorimotor coordination[8]. As a result, they fre-
quently induce visual–vestibular conflict, reduce immersion, and
constrain the user’s ability to observe and navigate simultaneously.
Furthermore, abstract inputs typically occupy the user’s hands, lim-
iting opportunities for interaction during flight-based tasks that
require both mobility and environmental awareness.
To improve naturalness and enhance perceptual freedom, re-
searchers have proposed embodied flight interfaces that rely on
body leaning or posture adjustments[26, 27]. These techniques in-
troduce body-related cues and can reduce discomfort relative to
abstract mappings[1]. However, most existing embodied flight sys-
tems depend on static input–output relationships that fail to accom-
modate the dynamic requirements of 3D locomotion. During natural
locomotion, users shift between low-speed exploration—requiring
broad, unconstrained head movements—and high-speed travel,
where tighter directional coupling becomes necessary. Static map-
pings cannot reconcile these opposing demands, often leading to
unintended activations, inconsistent directional control, or reduced
transparency.
This limitation points to a broader gap in current locomotion
research: effective embodied 3D locomotion requires control strate-
gies that adapt in ways consistent with human sensorimotor be-
havior. We refer to this as bio-congruent adaptation for embodied
3D locomotion. Such adaptation aligns system behavior with the
natural, context-dependent adjustments humans make when coor-
dinating gaze, head, and body during movement.
To implement this principle, we present AdaptiveFly, a 4-degree-
of-freedom embodied flight technique grounded in bio-congruent
adaptive mechanisms. AdaptiveFly dynamically adjusts the cou-
pling between head rotation and yaw control based on the user’s
current speed: at low speeds, the system weakens this coupling to
support free and uninterrupted visual exploration; at high speeds,
it strengthens the coupling to maintain intuitive and responsive
steering. Additionally, the system automatically calibrates control
sensitivity to the user’s individual posture characteristics, reducing
unintentional input. An asymmetric pitch mapping founded on hu-
man gaze biomechanics further enhances comfort and intuitiveness
during vertical movements. Together, these components instan-
tiate bio-congruent adaptation within a practical 3D locomotion
technique, enabling users to explore virtual environments freely
while maintaining a fluid and intuitive sense of movement. Our
contributions are as follows:
•We introduce AdaptiveFly, an embodied, context-adaptive
3D flight technique that applies the concept of bio-congruent
adaptation for embodied 3D locomotion and supports free
visual exploration during movement.
•Through empirical evaluation, we demonstrate its experi-
ential benefits over abstract and static embodied baselines
in terms of naturalness, comfort, and coordination between
looking and steering.
•Based on our findings, we provide design implications
for human-augmentation and 3D user interface research,
highlighting the importance of context-adaptive and bio-
congruent control in achieving natural embodied locomo-
tion.
2 Related Works
2.1 3D Locomotion in Virtual Environments
Locomotion techniques in virtual environments have seen diverse
technical approaches proposed to address the requirement of ex-
ploring vast virtual spaces within finite physical spaces. Techniques
such as redirected walking [22] and omnidirectional treadmills [16]
enable locomotion across vast VR spaces within small physical
spaces by altering the correspondence between the user’s walking
actions and actual physical displacement. Additionally, methods
applying motion-based walking metaphors that can be performed
while remaining stationary, such as Walking-in-Place (WIP) [ 3]
and arm-swinging techniques [25], have also been proposed. These
methods are all characterized by their ability to reference the eco-
logical standard of real-world walking. In contrast, VR affords the
possibility of designing three-dimensional locomotion techniques

that would be impossible in the physical world. However, the lack
of innate mental models for such modalities complicates the design
strategy of natural interfaces [29]. Some 3D locomotion methods
intentionally embrace physically impossible or non-ecological in-
teraction metaphors. Techniques such as Teleportation enhance
task efficiency and usability but disrupt spatial continuity, often
resulting in disorientation [6]. World-in-Miniature (WIM) allows
users to traverse vast distances instantly or manipulate their po-
sition from a god-like perspective, but it introduces an exocentric
viewpoint that can break embodied continuity and requires addi-
tional cognitive effort to reconcile multiple scales of representation
[34].
Parallel to these developments, many locomotion techniques
can be organized along a spectrum of motor input abstraction. At
one end of this spectrum are abstract controller-based interfaces
that treat physical motions as symbolic commands. Joystick-based
locomotion, for example, offers precision and low fatigue but tends
to induce cybersickness due to visual-vestibular conflict [8]. At the
opposite end are physically based mappings, in which metaphor-
ical interpretations of bodily movements are directly coupled to
locomotion behaviors. Examples include bird-like flapping gestures
[24] or grab-and-pull interactions [30]. While such embodied tech-
niques are reported to enhance the sense of agency and immersion,
they are associated with difficulties in precise manipulation and
significant physical load and fatigue [1]. Between these extremes
lie hybrid techniques that blend physical metaphors with abstract
control properties. For example, Leaning techniques that map trunk
inclination to propulsion [26, 27] and Gaze-directed techniques that
steer movement in the direction of gaze [5] retain physical move-
ment metaphors while possessing control characteristics similar
to abstract inputs such as joysticks. Multiple studies suggest that
these methods may contribute to mitigating cybersickness due to
the relatively high congruence between visual motion and postu-
ral changes[1, 26]. Although no universal “gold standard” exists
for 3D locomotion, research is progressing toward identifying an
optimal balance along the spectrum —leveraging appropriately ab-
stracted physical movements to achieve comfortable, immersive,
and intuitively controllable 3D locomotion.
2.2 Coupling of Body Motion in Motion-based
Locomotion
While locomotion techniques that incorporate physical movement
generally offer greater intuitiveness and immersion than abstract
controller-based inputs, they face a structural limitation: the natural
movement of body parts becomes intrinsically and continuously
coupled with the system’s locomotion control. This tight coupling
risks misinterpreting exploratory or task-related movements as
locomotion commands.
Gaze-oriented locomotion, which uses HMD orientation or eye
gaze as the travel vector, has become a default choice in many VR
applications due to ecological evidence that head and gaze typically
precede the direction of travel [2, 11]. However, because this method
algorithmically equates gaze direction with intended movement
direction, it inhibits the natural behavior of “looking around while
walking forward, ” producing what is known as the Look–Steer Con-
flict [4]. This issue has the same root as the Midas Touch problem,
where "looking constitutes selection" [ 17], and results from the
system’s over-interpretation of gaze as an indicator of locomotor
intent. A conventional solution is to introduce a dead zone—an
interval in which the system does not respond until gaze devia-
tion exceeds a predefined threshold. Nonlinear control schemes
have also been shown to alleviate this issue [ 9]. Other counter-
measures include suppressing unintended actuation by gaze input
through association with different body parts. For example, tech-
niques requiring the alignment of both gaze and hand directions
for steering allow directional input to be decoupled from visual
attention [37]. Other approaches modify the body part responsible
for steering. Regarding the comparison with hand-based steering,
results are inconsistent: some studies suggest that gaze-directed
steering is superior [35], while others report contrary results [4].
Alternatively, torso-based steering techniques have demonstrated
improvements in naturalness, efficiency, and maneuverability [38].
And recent work shows that leaning-based steering, which is also
a torso-based steering, can outperform hand-based control during
dual-task situations, reducing interference between locomotion and
manual interaction and enhancing overall performance and user
experience [13]. However, these approaches primarily substitute
one input modality for another and do not address the underlying
limitation: physical movements used for perception, balance, or
task interaction remain statically coupled with the system’s control
parameters. Because user intent — whether to explore the environ-
ment visually or to initiate locomotion — fluctuates dynamically
with context, any static mapping risks recurrent conflicts between
natural bodily behavior and system interpretation.
2.3 Adaptive Motion Coupling in Locomotion
In human locomotor control, the coordination dynamics among
the eyes, head, and torso are not static but constitute a hierarchi-
cally organized system that is dynamically reconfigured depending
on the required range of motion. According to Saeb et al., gaze
shifts smaller than 20° are accomplished primarily through ocular
movements, and the contribution of the head remains relatively
small—approximately one third—up to around 30°[32]. Furthermore,
Sidenmark et al. reported that the torso contributes substantially to
rotational behavior only during large-amplitude gaze shifts exceed-
ing 100°[33]. These findings indicate the existence of a functional
decoupling zone in which the torso operates mechanically inde-
pendently from gaze direction during small-amplitude exploratory
behaviors. During high-speed locomotion or turning maneuvers,
however, this coordination relationship transitions toward anticipa-
tory coupling. In such contexts, trajectory prediction takes prece-
dence over gaze stabilization, and both the head and torso rotate or
tilt toward the forthcoming movement trajectory to lead the body’s
heading. As demonstrated by Imai et al., the gaze can substantially
precede the heading direction at high angular velocities[15], and the
orientation of the head is controlled to align with the gravitational-
inertial acceleration (GIA) vector.
In contrast, virtual reality (VR) environments have been shown
to reorganize these natural coordination patterns, often resulting in
dynamics that diverge from those observed in the real world. Pfeil
et al. found that VR users rely more heavily on head movements
than on ocular movements when orienting toward targets[ 28],

while Horsak et al. reported that the mere use of a head-mounted
display (HMD) increases mediolateral torso variability and in-
duces posture-coupling patterns that differ from those in physical
environments[14]. Speed-dependent coordination also exhibits
VR-specific alterations: Giphart et al. demonstrated that low-speed
walking in VR destabilizes arm–leg coordination, causing a prema-
ture transition to 1:1 coupling compared to natural walking[ 10].
Brument et al. further showed that when VR navigation methods
violate the natural speed–curvature relationship (the 1/3 power
law), the magnitude and timing of anticipatory reorientation of
the eyes, head, and shoulders differ markedly from those during
real-world locomotion[7].
Taken together, real-world locomotion exhibits a hierarchical
coordination structure in which the relationship among gaze, head,
and torso transitions flexibly and continuously between decoupling
and coupling depending on movement amplitude and locomotor
speed. In VR, however, these natural coordination structures may
be reorganized in ways that diverge from physical behavior, and it
remains unclear to what extent real-world coordination dynamics
and threshold structures generalize to VR settings[23]. Moreover,
VR display characteristics and locomotion interfaces can directly in-
fluence users’ perception of affordances, potentially altering the in-
terdependence of gaze control, postural regulation, and self-motion
perception[12]. Therefore, achieving bio-congruent 3D locomo-
tion in VR requires integrating the speed-dependent coordination
principles and natural threshold structures observed in real-world
behavior into the underlying VR control mechanisms.
3 Methodology
Our core design philosophy is to mimic the natural decoupling zone
found in human physiology. Research indicates that gaze shifts un-
der 20◦are purely ocular, while the head contributes significantly
up to 30◦before the torso engages [32]. Unlike previous methods
that rotate the virtual body linearly with the head, AdaptiveFly in-
troduces a non-linear control manifold. We suppress torso rotation
for head yaws within the ±30◦range, creating a stable zone for
visual inspection without unintended steering.
Beyond static thresholds, we incorporate the anticipatory mech-
anism observed in high-velocity locomotion [15]. We formulated a
dynamic coupling factor 𝑘 that increases with the user’s angular
velocity. As the user turns their head faster, the system predicts
a trajectory change and actively rotates the virtual torso into the
turn, aligning the view with the Gravito-Inertial Acceleration (GIA)
vector.
3.1 Personalized CoP Translation
A primary limitation of static leaning-based interfaces is their “one-
size-fits-all” approach. They impose a fixed force threshold (e.g.,
a 5cm lean) that completely ignores the significant inter-person
variability in natural postural sway. This makes the interface feel
unresponsive for some users and overly sensitive for others.
To address this, we introduce a personalized calibration mech-
anism. This system is founded on the first of our bio-congruent
principles: the system must first understand the user’s unique, nat-
ural body dynamics. During a brief calibration phase (approx. 30
seconds), the user is asked to stand still and relaxed on the force plat-
form. We capture the Center of Pressure (CoP) data and model the
user’s natural sway distribution as a bi-variate Gaussian (N𝑠𝑤𝑎𝑦).

tailored to each user. The relationship between this model and the

3.2 Context-Adaptive Yaw Control
The second critical limitation of static interfaces is the “Look vs.
Steer” conflict. When stationary or moving slowly, users will want
to decouple their head rotation from their body’s direction to ob-
serve the environment. Conversely, when moving quickly, they
would want their head and body to be tightly coupled to steer.
Static mappings cannot accommodate both, breaking transparency.
Our solution is inspired by human locomotion, which is highly
context-dependent. Studies show that during locomotion, a change
in direction is tightly coupled with a preceding head rotation, and
this coupling becomes much stronger than at rest.AdaptiveFly mir-
rors this bio-congruence. We propose a Context-Adaptive Yaw con-
trol that dynamically adjusts the coupling between head rotation
and yaw rate based on the user’s current velocity.
The control manifold is illustrated in Figure 2 (b). This mecha-
nism transparently supports two distinct modes:
•Low-speed exploration: When velocity 𝑣𝑑 is low, the sys-
tem uses a large deadzone (𝜇𝑚𝑎𝑥 ≈30◦). This allows the user
to freely look around without causing unintended steering.

reduces the deadzone to a small value ( 𝜇𝑚𝑖𝑛 ≈12◦). This

3.3 Asymmetric Pitch Control
Finally, to enhance comfort and naturalness during vertical move-
ment (the 3rd and 4th DoF), we implemented a third bio-congruent
mechanism for elevation control, mapped to the user’s head pitch.
AdaptiveFly’s elevation control utilizes an asymmetric map-
ping. This design is motivated by physiological studies on human
gaze, which show that the natural contribution of head movement
is smaller for downwards gazeshifts compared to those directed
upwards[20, 33].

be less sensitive for downward head pitches (𝜃ℎ) than for upward
pitches. This aligns with the natural kinematic constraints of the
neck and mitigates uncomfortable or extreme movements, particu-
larly when looking down.

4 Experiment
To validate the effectiveness of AdaptiveFly, we conducted a user
study designed to evaluate its performance, motion quality, and
user experience across different flight tasks. We compared Adap-
tiveFly against traditional joystick control and two ablated mapping
embodied flight baselines.
4.1 Participants
We recruited 12 participants (6 female, 6 male). The mean age of the
participants was 25.5 years (𝑆𝐷 = 2.62). The participants possessed
varying levels of VR and gaming experience, ranging from zero
to over 20 years. Two additional participants withdrew from the
study during the first round of experiments due to severe cybersick-
ness; their data were excluded from the analysis. All participants
had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were informed of the
experimental procedures and potential risks, and provided written
informed consent. The experimental protocol was approved by the
local Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. [E2025ALS216-01]).
4.2 Apparatus
The experiment was conducted in an environment equipped with a
high-performance PC, using the Unity 6 engine to render the virtual
scenes. Participants wore a Meta Quest 3 Head-Mounted Display
(HMD) for immersive visual feedback and head motion tracking. We
used a force plate to measure changes in the participant’s Center of
Pressure (CoP), implemented using the Flexel modular floor system
AHs 2026, March 16–19, 2026, Okinawa, Japan Kong et al.
[36]. In our setup, Flexel provided a 6 ×6 grid of sensing cells (36
cells in total), each derived from quad-pod load sensors, and the
load distribution was sampled at 80 Hz. Additionally, a standard
gamepad joystick was used as the baseline condition for abstract
control.
4.3 Study Design and Conditions
The study employed a within-subjects design with Locomotion
Mode as the independent variable. To rigorously evaluate the con-
tribution of our adaptive mechanism, we compared our method
against three baselines:
•AdaptiveFly (Our Method): Embodied control utilizing a
Context-Adaptive Yaw/Pitch coupling mechanism.
•CoupledFly (Fully Coupled): A 1:1 "Gaze-to-Steer" embod-
ied control, serving as an ablation baseline with the adaptive
mechanism disabled (coupling strength fixed at 𝛼 = 1.0).
•StaticFly (Fixed Threshold): Embodied control using fixed
thresholds for rotation and pitch. Steering and elevation are
activated via a discrete mechanism: exceeding a 30◦head
rotation deadzone triggers a constant 60◦/𝑠 turn rate and
maximum vertical velocity.
•Joystick (Baseline): Control via a standard gamepad, serv-
ing as a baseline for abstract control.
In all embodied control modes, horizontal movement was con-
trolled by CoP displacement (body lean) on the force plate.
4.4 Tasks
To comprehensively evaluate the performance of different locomo-
tion conditions across scenarios, we designed two distinct tasks
covering navigation and observation:
•Task 1. Helix Navigation (Precision): Participants were
required to fly along a predefined helical path marked by a se-
ries of dots. This task aimed to assess precise path-following
capabilities, as shown in Figure 3.
•Task 2. Orbit Observation (Stability): Participants were
required to orbit around a central target while keeping their
gaze fixed on it. This task evaluated the ability to decouple
view direction from travel direction (resolving the Look-
Steer conflict).
4.5 Procedure
The experimental session began with an explanation of the study’s
purpose and the signing of informed consent forms, followed by
the completion of a baseline cybersickness questionnaire. Next, a
personalized CoP calibration was performed: participants stood
still on the force plate to calculate their sway distribution and then
leaned in four cardinal directions to establish their natural range of
motion. To minimize bias, participants were not explicitly informed
of the specific locomotion mechanics unless they failed to discover
the control rules within two minutes.
Subsequently, they entered a practice scene containing static
objects like rings and columns and a flock of dynamic fish-like
boid agents based on the model by Reynolds [31], implemented by
Lague [21]. Participants practiced by freely chasing the boids or
navigating through obstacles until they reported proficiency. Once
familiarized, they completed two tasks under each condition; the
order was counterbalanced using a 4 ×4 Latin Square. The entire
procedure is illustrated in Figure 3. Finally, after each condition,
participants filled out subjective assessment questionnaires (cy-
bersickness questionnaire first), with mandatory breaks enforced
between blocks.
4.6 Data Collection and Metrics
We collected both objective performance metrics and subjective
assessment data. For objective metrics, system data was recorded at
60 Hz and pre-filtered. We analyzed Motion Quality via vertical and
angular jerk (RMS) to assess smoothness, while Task Performance
was evaluated by completion time for Task 1 and gaze offset stabil-
ity (RMSE) for Task 2. Regarding subjective metrics, we employed
a modified Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) [ 18]. To bal-
ance sensitivity with efficiency, we selected items covering Nausea,
Oculomotor, and Disorientation; this ensured the capture of gastric
symptoms induced by continuous locomotion—often omitted by
the shorter VRSQ [ 19]—while avoiding less relevant autonomic
symptoms to minimize participant fatigue. Additionally, we mea-
sured cognitive load using raw NASA-TLX, Sense of Embodiment
(SoE) (Agency, Bodily Sensation, Self-Location), and custom ratings
for interaction transparency, flow, and enjoyment.

scores, the data met normality assumptions; therefore, we used
a repeated-measures ANOVA and report the corresponding𝐹 statis-

5.3 Qualitative Feedback
Participant interview feedback provided valuable context for the
quantitative results and revealed the pros and cons of different
control modes.
AdaptiveFly (Balance & Focus): Many participants felt Adap-
tiveFly achieved a good balance between comfort and control. P4
felt AdaptiveFly was "just right. " In the observation task (Task 2),
AdaptiveFly was particularly favored (P1, P2, P10). P5 noted that
with AdaptiveFly, they could best "immerse in observation" and
"focus on the task itself, " whereas other modes required "figuring
out how to control the viewpoint. " P10 considered AdaptiveFly "the
most comfortable" and "probably the best overall. "
CoupledFly (Speed vs. Error): CoupledFly was appreciated
for its high responsiveness and speed, especially in tasks requiring
quick reactions. P1 thought it "moved very smoothly, suitable for
Task 1. " However, its high sensitivity was also a major drawback. P4
felt it was "too sensitive. " P10 noted it was "too easy to misoperate;
turning the head makes you dizzy easily. " This drawback was par-
ticularly evident in the observation task Task2, with P2 explicitly
stating that CoupledFly was "the hardest to use" in Task2.
StaticFly (Lag & Discomfort): StaticFly was generally crit-
icized, with many participants reporting severe discomfort and
control difficulties (P1, P9, P11 all stated StaticFly was the dizziest).
P3 felt "frame drops and a sense of lag, easy to lose control of direc-
tion. " This "disconnection between body movement and will" (P5)
was considered the primary cause of discomfort.
Joystick (High Load & Low Immersion): As a baseline, Joy-
stick was perceived as difficult to control with high cognitive load.
P2 and P10 rated Joystick extremely poorly. P5 felt Joystick made
them "focus on control rather than observation. " P12 also mentioned
that, apart from Joystick, the other modes "were all very immersive. "
6 Implementation to Physical Platform
While the previous study validated AdaptiveFly within a controlled
virtual environment, assessing the ecological validity of the system
requires deployment on physical hardware. In this section, we de-
scribe a proof-of-concept implementation where the control scheme
was transposed onto a commercial Vertical Take-Off and Landing
(VTOL) quadcopter. This deployment aimed to verify the techni-
cal feasibility of the architecture and identify critical hardware
requirements for embodied drone flight.
6.1 System Integration
We utilized a DJI Tello Ryze quadcopter as the physical platform.
The interface architecture remained identical to the VR implemen-
tation (see Figure 8). Regarding control mapping, the velocity com-
mands generated by the virtual drone model were scaled down by
a constant factor and transmitted to the physical drone via Wi-Fi;
to ensure safety, the velocity vector magnitude was capped at 10
cm/s. The yaw rate was mapped in a 1:1 ratio, ensuring the drone’s
heading strictly followed the user’s head rotation. Finally, for visual
feedback, the video stream (720p, 30 FPS) from the drone’s fixed

front-facing camera was retrieved and rendered directly into the
user’s HMD to provide first-person view (FPV) feedback.
6.2 Field Evaluation
We conducted a qualitative evaluation using a "Fly Through Way-
points" (FTW) task. Users were asked to navigate the drone through
physical hoops (diameter ≈70 cm) spaced five meters apart and
positioned 1-2 meters above the ground (Figure 8).

6.3 Observations and Challenges
Technically, the system successfully translated body-based inputs
into drone actuation with minimal latency. However, piloting the
physical drone proved significantly more cognitively demanding
than the simulation. This pilot study revealed two critical hardware
constraints that currently hinder the full potential of embodied
flight in real-world settings:
The consumer-grade camera provided a low-resolution, monoc-
ular video stream. Unlike the 3D rendered environment in VR,
this 2D feed lacked binocular depth cues, making distance estima-
tion challenging. Furthermore, the restricted Field of View (FOV
≈82.6◦) meant users lost visual contact with the hoops up to 50 cm
before traversal. This forced users to rely on optical flow estimation
and mental trajectory extrapolation rather than continuous visual
feedback, significantly increasing cognitive load.
A more fundamental issue arose from the lack of a mechanical
gimbal. In our embodied scheme, users look up (pitch up) to com-
mand ascent. However, since the camera is fixed to the drone’s
chassis, the visual horizon does not tilt up with the user’s head;
it only tilts if the drone physically pitches, which is often decou-
pled from vertical velocity in quadcopters. This created a sensory
conflict: the user’s proprioception signaled "looking up, " but the
visual feedback remained level. This mismatch disrupted the Sense
of Embodiment and spatial orientation, particularly during pure
elevation changes. Users had to actively suppress the expectation
that the camera would follow their head pitch, requiring careful
motion planning.
This transposition confirms that AdaptiveFly is technically ag-
nostic to the target platform (virtual or physical). However, the
qualitative results strongly suggest that for embodied control to
be effective in the real world, the drone hardware must match
the sensory fidelity of VR. Specifically, a stereoscopic camera (for
depth) and a synchronized 2-axis gimbal (to align camera pitch
with head pitch) are prerequisites to resolving the sensory conflicts
we observed.
7 Discussion
The primary goal of this study was to determine if a bio-congruent
approach could resolve the tension between high performance and
the sense of embodiment in VR locomotion. We proposed Adap-
tiveFly, a 4-DoF embodied flight paradigm, to test this hypothesis.
Our results achieved both high task performance and a strong self-
localization, while significantly improving user comfort.
Our findings highlight the inherent limitations of static map-
pings. CoupledFly, which uses a fixed Gaze-to-Steer mapping, per-
formed as expected in the precision task (Task 1) where looking
and steering are naturally aligned. However, this advantage came
at a significant cost to stability. In the observation task (Task 2),
CoupledFly performed the worst, showing much higher gaze offset
than all other conditions. This confirms that when a user wants to
look around without turning, a fully coupled system creates severe
interference.
AdaptiveFly, by comparison, proved flexible enough to handle
these conflicting task demands. In precision task (Task 1), it per-
formed nearly as well as CoupledFly and significantly better than
the Joystick. More importantly, it outperformed all baselines in the
observation task. This features directly from our core contribution:
Context-Adaptive Yaw control. When flying fast, the system tight-
ens the coupling for precision; at low speeds, it loosens the control,
allowing users to look around freely. As noted by Participant 5, this
makes the control feel "transparent, " letting users focus on the task
rather than the interface.
Looking at the motion quality metrics helps explain why Adap-
tiveFly felt better to use. A key finding was that CoupledFly resulted
in extremely high angular velocity and jerk—often double that of
other modes. This suggests that users were constantly fighting
the system, making rapid corrections to counteract unintended
steering caused by their head movements. In contrast, AdaptiveFly
produced the smoothest motion profile with the lowest jerk. The
AdaptiveFly: Bio-Congruent Adaptation for Embodied 3DLocomotion AHs 2026, March 16–19, 2026, Okinawa, Japan
adaptive deadzone effectively filtered out involuntary head move-
ments, making steering intentional and precise. We believe this
smoothness is a major factor in reducing cybersickness. Indeed,
AdaptiveFly yielded the lowest symptom scores, particularly for
dizziness and blurred vision. Conversely, the poor performance
of StaticFly (which used fixed thresholds) led to the most severe
discomfort. Participant feedback about "lag" and "stuttering" in the
StaticFly condition suggests that violating natural motion expecta-
tions, even with an embodied interface can be worse than using a
simple abstract controller.
Our physical drone deployment (Section 6) served as a "reality
check" for the ecological validity of our method. While the control
logic transferred successfully from VR to the real robot, the user
experience highlighted the critical role of sensory fidelity. We ob-
served a "Pitch-Gaze" conflict: users would pitch their heads up to
fly up, but the drone’s fixed camera remained level. This mismatch
broke the sense of embodiment and increased cognitive load. This
reinforces our argument for bio-congruence: embodied interfaces
work best when visual feedback matches vestibular and proprio-
ceptive expectations. Future physical implementations will need
hardware that matches human anatomy, such as a synchronized
gimbal.
Despite the significant advantages demonstrated by Adaptive-
Fly, this study has limitations. First, our sample size (𝑁 = 12) was
relatively small; future research could validate these findings with
a larger and more diverse sample. Second, while our physical de-
ployment demonstrated technical feasibility, the current lack of
stereoscopic vision and mechanical gimbals on the test drone lim-
ited the evaluation of complex maneuvers. Future work should eval-
uate AdaptiveFly on robotic platforms equipped with synchronized
2-axis gimbals and stereo cameras to fully replicate the sensory
fidelity of the VR condition. Furthermore, AdaptiveFly’s current
adaptive mechanism is primarily based on velocity as context in-
formation. Future work could explore integrating more biological
signals, such as eye-tracking data, to more accurately infer user
observation intent.
8 Conclusion
Traditional static mapping methods in VR locomotion often strug-
gle to balance high performance with natural embodiment. To ad-
dress this, we propose AdaptiveFly, which introduces bio-congruent
adaptive mechanisms to reconcile these demands. By integrating
Context-Adaptive Yaw and Personalized CoP calibration, the sys-
tem dynamically adjusts control strategies based on the interaction
context, effectively resolving the persistent ’Look vs. Steer’ conflict.
Through a comprehensive user study, we demonstrated that
AdaptiveFly not only maintains high performance in precision
navigation and dynamic efficiency tasks but also significantly out-
performs existing methods in tasks requiring independent observa-
tion and stability. Simultaneously, AdaptiveFly provides smoother
motion quality, enhances the user’s Self-Location, and reduces Cy-
bersickness. Furthermore, our implementation on a commercial
quadcopter confirms that this bio-congruent paradigm is platform-
agnostic, capable of controlling not just virtual avatars but also
physical robots, provided that sensory feedback aligns with user
expectations. Our research highlights the importance of context
adaptation in achieving transparent control and offers new direc-
tions for the future design of embodied 3D user interfaces.
